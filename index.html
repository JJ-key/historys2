U.S. History Fair Presentation

The Human Cost
of Powerful Weapons

Compelling Question

Should the United States think about how many people might get hurt — the human cost — when using powerful weapons to end a war?

Throughout history, the U.S. has deployed some of the most destructive weapons ever made. This presentation examines what happened to the ordinary people in the path of those weapons — and argues the U.S. has consistently failed to ask that question.

Scroll to explore

Our Argument

A pattern repeated across three eras of American military history

The U.S. government failed to consider the human cost when using powerful weapons to end wars, and should start doing so — because these weapons cause permanent environmental damage, devastate civilian communities, waste American resources, and damage the global reputation of the United States.

I
Environmental Damage
Radiation, chemical contamination, and destroyed land that persists for generations.
II
Civilian Devastation
Ordinary people — not soldiers — bear the greatest cost in every case examined.
III
Wasted Resources
The long-term cost of rebuilding, medical care, and diplomacy far exceeds the short-term gain.
IV
Global Reputation
Mass civilian casualties make it harder for the U.S. to build alliances and trust worldwide.

Era 01  |  1914 – 1945

The Atomic Bombs of Hiroshima and Nagasaki

August 6 and 9, 1945 — Japan

In August 1945, the United States dropped two atomic bombs on the Japanese cities of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. The U.S. government said the bombs were necessary to end World War II quickly and avoid a land invasion that could have killed even more people. But the numbers reveal a different story about who actually paid the price.

0
People killed — estimates range from 140,000 to 200,000
90%
Of those killed were civilians, not soldiers
0
Children killed in the two bombings
  • The cities were erased instantly. Both Hiroshima and Nagasaki were destroyed in seconds — buildings, hospitals, schools, and homes all gone.
  • Radiation kept killing for decades. Survivors developed cancer, leukemia, and severe illness long after the bombs fell.
  • Children were the most vulnerable. 38,000 children died. Many more were exposed to radiation that affected their health for life.
  • The environment was permanently changed. The land, water, and air around both cities carried radiation that could not be undone.

"With 90% of the dead being civilians, the bombs did not distinguish between soldiers and the people they were supposed to be protecting."

The U.S. justified the bombs by saying they would end the war faster and prevent more deaths overall. But no serious planning was done to reduce civilian casualties — no warning to evacuate cities, no targeting of military-only facilities. The human cost was not part of the calculation.

Sources: International Campaign to Abolish Nuclear Weapons (ICAN) — icanw.org  |  Britannica — britannica.com
Era 02  |  1946 – 1990

Agent Orange and Napalm in Vietnam

Vietnam War — 1955 to 1975, with effects lasting decades after

During the Vietnam War, the United States did not only use conventional weapons. It used two powerful chemical agents — napalm and Agent Orange — that caused suffering which did not end when the war ended. This era shows that the human cost of powerful weapons can last for entire generations.

Napalm

A fire that could not be stopped

Napalm is a burning chemical designed to stick to surfaces — including human skin. It burns at up to 2,200 degrees Fahrenheit, hot enough to melt metal. The U.S. dropped it on Vietnamese villages to clear areas quickly. It did not discriminate between soldiers and civilians.

Agent Orange

A poison that entered the food chain

Agent Orange is a toxic herbicide the U.S. sprayed over 5 million acres of Vietnamese land to destroy forests where enemy forces could hide. But it also destroyed farmland, contaminated water supplies, and entered the bodies of millions of civilians living in those areas.

0

Generations
Children born after the war still suffer birth defects and illness today

Agent Orange caused cancer, leukemia, and severe birth defects — not just in people who were directly exposed, but in their children and grandchildren. The damage passed from parent to child to grandchild. This is what it means when the human cost of war lasts generations.

  • The forests damaged by Agent Orange have never fully recovered, affecting Vietnam's environment to this day.
  • Soil contamination from dioxin — a chemical in Agent Orange — persists in parts of Vietnam more than 50 years later.
  • The U.S. government did not conduct a full study of the human impact before deploying these chemicals on populated areas.
Source: National Library of Medicine / NCBI — ncbi.nlm.nih.gov
Era 03  |  1990 – Present

Drone Strikes During the War on Terror

Afghanistan, Pakistan, Yemen, Somalia, Iraq — after September 11, 2001

After the September 11, 2001 terrorist attacks, the United States launched the War on Terror. To hunt terrorist leaders in remote regions, the U.S. military used drone strikes — unmanned aircraft that fire missiles from high altitude, often many miles away from a pilot sitting at a screen. The U.S. argued that drones were a precise, modern solution that would reduce civilian deaths. The data tells a completely different story.

0

Minimum civilian deaths during the War on Terror

This figure from Brown University's Costs of War Project represents the documented minimum. The true number is likely higher.

  • Only 2% of drone strike victims were actual high-level militant leaders — the people the strikes were designed to eliminate.
  • The other 98% of victims were not the intended target. Many were ordinary civilians going about their lives.
  • Drone strikes hit wedding parties, funerals, markets, and homes — not just military compounds.
  • Communities live in constant fear from drones circling overhead. The psychological damage extends far beyond those physically harmed.

Modern technology did not solve the problem of civilian casualties. Even in the 21st century — with satellite guidance and precision engineering — the U.S. continued to kill thousands of civilians without adequate planning for the human cost.

The damage to the United States' reputation in the regions where it was trying to fight terrorism made it harder to gain local cooperation — making the War on Terror less effective, not more.

Addressing the Other Side

What about the argument that these weapons saved more lives than they cost?

This is a serious argument that deserves a fair response. Here is both the strongest version of the counterargument — and why the evidence still does not support it.

The Counterargument

The U.S. argued that dropping atomic bombs on Japan prevented a full land invasion that military planners estimated could have killed 500,000 to 1,000,000 American soldiers — and millions more Japanese. By that logic, 200,000 deaths prevented millions.


Similarly, drone strikes were supposed to eliminate terrorist leaders with far less risk to American soldiers than traditional ground operations, reducing overall casualties on both sides.

Why This Does Not Hold

Good intentions do not undo the evidence. Even accepting the best-case version of this argument, the actual outcome in every era was massive, long-lasting civilian harm that was never adequately planned for or minimized.


The assumption in each case was that there were no other options. But better planning, more targeted approaches, and serious consideration of the human cost could have significantly reduced civilian casualties — without abandoning the military objectives.


The question is not whether the U.S. had bad intentions. The question is whether it gave serious thought to how many ordinary people would be hurt. The evidence clearly shows it did not.

Conclusion

Three eras. Three different weapons. The same failure — every time.

The U.S. government failed to consider the human cost when using powerful weapons to end wars, and should start doing so — because these weapons cause permanent environmental damage, devastate civilian communities, waste American resources, and damage the global reputation of the United States.

Era 03  |  1990–Present

Drone Strikes

At least 408,749 civilians killed in the War on Terror. Only 2% of drone victims were the intended targets. Even modern "precise" weapons produced massive civilian harm.

The U.S. must start counting the human cost

The damage from powerful weapons does not end when the war ends. It affects civilians who never chose to fight, communities that take generations to rebuild, environments that may never fully recover, and the global standing of the United States itself. Considering the human cost before acting is not weakness — it is the responsibility that comes with being the most powerful military on earth.
