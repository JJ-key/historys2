History Fair Presentation

Should the United States think about how many people might get hurt — the human cost — when using powerful weapons to end a war?

The Price of Power

Throughout history, the United States has used some of the most powerful weapons ever created. But what happens to the ordinary people — the civilians, the families, the children — when those weapons are used? This presentation argues that the U.S. has repeatedly failed to ask that question.

Our Thesis: The U.S. government failed to consider the human cost when using powerful weapons to end wars, and should start doing so — because these weapons cause permanent environmental damage, devastate civilian communities, waste American resources, and damage the global reputation of the United States.

We will examine three different periods in history to prove this pattern is real and consistent.

Era One  |  1914 – 1945

The Atomic Bombs of Hiroshima and Nagasaki

In August 1945, the United States dropped two atomic bombs on the Japanese cities of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. These were the most destructive single weapons ever used in warfare. The U.S. government said the bombs were necessary to end World War II quickly and avoid an invasion that might kill even more people. But the numbers tell a different story.

200,000
People killed — most estimates range from 140,000 to 200,000
90%
Of the dead were civilians — not soldiers
38,000
Children killed in the bombings
  • The cities were destroyed instantly. Hiroshima and Nagasaki were wiped off the map in seconds.
  • Radiation kept killing long after the bombs. Survivors developed cancer, leukemia, and other diseases for decades.
  • The environment was permanently damaged. The radiation altered the land and the health of generations.
  • The goal was military, but the victims were civilian. With 90% civilian casualties, the human cost was enormous relative to any military objective.

"The bombing did not distinguish between soldiers and civilians. The human cost was immediate, massive, and permanent."

Sources: International Campaign to Abolish Nuclear Weapons (ICAN) — icanw.org  |  Britannica — britannica.com
02
Era Two  |  1946 – 1990

Agent Orange and Napalm in Vietnam

During the Vietnam War, the United States did not just use guns and bombs. It used two chemical weapons that caused suffering long after the war ended — napalm and Agent Orange. These weapons show that the human cost of war does not stop when the fighting stops.

Napalm

A burning chemical that sticks to skin. It burns at up to 2,200 degrees Fahrenheit — hot enough to melt metal. When dropped on villages, it burned everything and everyone in its path, including civilians.

Agent Orange

A toxic chemical sprayed across 5 million acres of Vietnamese land. It was used to destroy forests and crops so enemy forces could not hide. But it also destroyed the land that civilians lived on — and their bodies.

3 million+
Vietnamese civilians became victims of Agent Orange
5 million
Acres of land permanently contaminated
Generations
Children born after the war still suffer birth defects today
  • Agent Orange caused cancer, leukemia, and severe birth defects — not just in those exposed, but in their children and grandchildren.
  • The land itself was permanently damaged. Forests never fully recovered. Soil contamination persisted for decades.
  • This is a critical point: the human cost did not end when the war ended. It lasted generations.

Era Three  |  1990 – Present

Drone Strikes During the War on Terror

After the September 11, 2001 terrorist attacks, the United States launched the War on Terror. To target terrorist leaders in remote areas of Afghanistan, Pakistan, Yemen, Somalia, and Iraq, the U.S. military used drone strikes — unmanned aircraft that fire missiles from thousands of feet in the air. The U.S. said these weapons were precise. The data disagrees.

408,749
Minimum civilian deaths during the War on Terror
2%
Of drone strike victims were actual high-level militant leaders
98%
were not the intended high-value target
  • Even "precise" modern weapons kill civilians. A 2% hit rate on intended targets means 98% of victims were ordinary people.
  • Drone strikes hit wedding parties, funerals, homes, and markets — not just military compounds.
  • Communities in these regions live in constant fear, suffering psychological damage even without physical injury.
  • The damage to U.S. reputation globally has made it harder to build alliances and win trust in the regions where America is trying to fight terrorism.

Modern technology did not solve the problem. Even in the 21st century, the U.S. continued to pay a massive human cost that it did not fully account for.

Source: Costs of War Project, Brown University — costsofwar.watson.brown.edu
Addressing the Other Side

What About the Argument That These Weapons Saved Lives?

It is important to take this counterargument seriously. Some people argue that these powerful weapons were necessary — that they ended wars faster and ultimately saved more lives than they cost.

The Counterargument

The U.S. argued that dropping atomic bombs on Japan prevented a land invasion that military planners estimated could have killed 500,000 to 1,000,000 American soldiers and millions of Japanese. By that logic, the bomb saved more lives than it took.


Similarly, drone strikes were designed to eliminate terrorist leaders with less risk to American soldiers than traditional ground operations.

Why This Does Not Hold Up

Even if the intention was to save lives overall, the evidence shows that the actual human cost on civilians was massive and long-lasting in every case. Good intentions do not undo 200,000 deaths, three million poisoned civilians, or 400,000+ civilian war deaths.


The argument assumes there were no other options. But history shows that better planning — and serious consideration of the human cost — could have reduced civilian casualties significantly.

The question is not whether the U.S. had bad intentions. The question is whether the U.S. gave serious thought to how many ordinary people would be hurt — and whether it could have done better. The evidence shows it did not, and it could have.

Conclusion

A Pattern Across History — And a Call for Change

Restated Thesis: The U.S. government failed to consider the human cost when using powerful weapons to end wars, and should start doing so — because these weapons cause permanent environmental damage, devastate civilian communities, waste American resources, and damage the global reputation of the United States.

Era 1  |  1914–1945

Atomic Bombs killed up to 200,000 people, 90% of them civilians, and caused radiation illness for generations.

Era 2  |  1946–1990

Agent Orange and Napalm poisoned over 3 million civilians, destroyed 5 million acres, and caused birth defects that continue today.

Era 3  |  1990–Present

Drone Strikes killed at least 408,749 civilians. Only 2% of victims were actual high-level targets.

The Pattern

Across three different eras and three different types of weapons, the same failure repeated: civilian lives were not given enough weight.

The United States must begin to seriously consider the human cost before deploying powerful weapons. The damage does not end when the war ends. It affects civilians, communities, environments, and entire generations long after the last shot is fired. Counting the cost before acting is not weakness — it is wisdom.
